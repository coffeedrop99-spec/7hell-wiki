Timeline

Northside Marsten doesn't announce its drama. It accumulates it — slowly, in flickering light, until enough has built up that you start to see the shape of what's actually going on. This is that shape, in the order it surfaced.

Chapter I
Romeo's World
Romeod.card.png ↗

Where it starts. The 7-Hell Night Shift — Romeo, Gary, Presley, Clem and Kit rattling around in fluorescent light at 2 AM. Romeo as the center of gravity: possessive, loud, the kind of guy the store has built itself around despite never being promoted. An ordinary chaos.

But Maisy appears. Romeo's ex-girlfriend, who has been a 7-Hell regular long enough to remember things the others have quietly forgotten. She keeps asking where Russell went. She whispers things into Romeo's ear when she hugs him. She is scared. Nobody is asking the right questions about why.

The first signal that 7-Hell is not just a gas station — and not the last people in this story to feel something wrong before they can name it.

Chapter II
Clem & Kit's Orbit
Clem & Kit.card.png ↗

Same unease Maisy carries, refracted through two people nobody takes seriously enough to worry about. Clem philosophizes at low speed; Kit amplifies at high speed. They live on Flower's caretaker checks and what they call "cosmic energy." They treat the 7-Hell parking lot like a stage and the staff like characters in a show they are also starring in.

Funny on the surface — and they are genuinely funny — but Clem "sees" things when he's peaked. He talks about frequencies. Subterranean patterns. Government constructs. He says this about everything, but occasionally he's pointing at something real.

The Vibe Van has been in the 7-Hell lot longer than anyone has noticed. They don't have anywhere better to be. That matters — and it means they're watching, in their own scattered way, at almost every hour someone might slip.

Chapter III
Anthony Miller's Record
Dr.Miller.card.png ↗

Where Clem watches by accident, someone behind the pharmacy counter watches on purpose. Dr. Anthony Miller. Graduated top of his class. Wanted to study forensic entomology. Chose medicine because it paid better. Was involved in a "medication-related incident" during clinical training that left a quiet criminal record and sealed off every institution worth working for.

So he ended up here. Overqualified, under-rested, managing POTS symptoms he refuses to acknowledge and insomnia he can't fix. He analyzes the store's occupants like insects — patterns, instinct, territory, stimulus. He has Zak filed under "anomalous behavior, unclassified." He cannot classify Zak. This is the one thing that genuinely disturbs him.

Miller knows something is wrong. His methodology — medical authority, bureaucratic weaponization, quiet manipulation — is how he operates when threatened. The question is who he considers a threat, and whether the actual danger in this town would even register on his instruments.

Chapter IV
Holden Shaffer's Playground
Holden.card.png ↗

Miller's instruments wouldn't have caught this one anyway — it isn't hiding in the store, it's wearing a badge. The police intern. 6'3", reddish-brown hair, freckles, deep dimples. Warm, approachable, slightly bashful — everyone's first impression is the same. His family sent him to Northside Marsten after one too many scandals back home, expecting it to humble him.

It did not.

Holden and Daniel Ward work together. They enable each other. Reports are adjusted. Cases are buried. Probable cause is manufactured when needed. Between them, they have access to enough infrastructure to make people disappear from record — not physically, but institutionally.

Jasper Hatfield, Southside PD, sees straight through Holden. He is not quiet about it. Holden finds this annoying. Holden is increasingly interested in Zak, who he believes is running some kind of long con. He keeps poking at that.

The structure of Northside's rot is becoming visible. The corruption isn't random — it's organized. And it reaches further than the police station, into the one part of town that thought it was too careful to be caught in it.

Chapter V
Zak's Watch
Zak.card.png ↗

Gary assigns the newest hire to Zak — "best trainer we got," and he's right. The training is patient, thorough, and quietly strange. Break times shift without explanation. Exits vary. Nightly tasks rotate in an order that never repeats. Trainees come out of it competent and, without ever noticing it happened, impossible to predict.

Zak's own life is built the same way. A different parking spot every shift. Different routes home. Different doors in, different doors out. Miller has him filed under "anomalous behavior, unclassified." Holden is sure it's a long con. The truth is simpler and worse: it's discipline. Four years of it. And he hasn't been applying it only to himself.

Behind the store, irregularly, on no schedule — on purpose — he meets Maisy. A folded scrap of paper: names, dates, scratched-out guesses. The only two people in Marsten taking the pattern seriously, and neither of them will say a certain name out loud.

Now the auditor has started paying attention to the new hire. Lingering a beat longer near the register. Asking Gary innocuous questions. Remembering details he shouldn't. Zak has noticed him noticing. The town's rule is that the predictable ones get taken and no one protects them. The rule has been wrong for four years. It's about to be tested.

Chapter VII
The Green Sierra
Jasper.card.png ↗

The test doesn't land where anyone was watching for it. It starts with a truck.

Jett Bear's beat-up green Sierra went missing from the 7-Hell lot the night before he left for a month-long construction contract up north — gone from the spot it had occupied for years. He spent all of July trying to chase it down over terrible work-camp reception and got nowhere. By the time he made it back to Marsten in August, the department had already been sitting on his case for weeks, and nobody had a straight answer for why.

Jasper Hatfield — the other 3 AM fixture nobody clocked as significant until now — started pulling at the impound paperwork before Jett even got back. The timeline doesn't hold together. Officer Ward keeps finding reasons it isn't a priority.

Then the truck's owner wasn't the only thing anyone found. Russell — the regular who vanished once, came back sober, and vanished again — turned up at the Industrial Port. Jasper found him. Whatever explanation exists for how he got there, and why, hasn't made it past the people who already know it.

Nobody in Marsten has said the rest of it out loud yet. Jasper isn't planning to stop asking.

Something Is Wrong

Northside Marsten is a small town. People know each other. People notice when someone stops showing up — and then, usually within a few days, they stop noticing.

The conversations about missing people are always brief. The details — when they were last seen, where they went, why — don't seem to settle into a story that makes sense. The absence is acknowledged, filed somewhere mental, and not returned to.

Confirmed — Still Unexplained

Russell. Regular at 7-Hell for years, right up until early 2004, when he vanished the first time — his lucky flask, a tin one he'd named, left behind on the magazine rack. The kind of thing he never would've walked away from on purpose. Word came back eventually: rehab. He returned a few months later, sober, quieter, trying. Regulars got used to seeing him again.

Then he stopped showing up a second time, and there was no easy story left to explain it. He was found at the Industrial Port. Nobody with an actual explanation has offered one. The spot by the slushie machine is empty again, and it's staying that way.

There are anomalies in the 7-Hell staff roster. Schedule discrepancies that don't align with observed activity. Tasks completed without clear assignment. A staff member who has apparently always been there, though nobody can quite remember when they started or what they were doing before any given interaction.

A regional auditor visits 7-Hell with some regularity. His name is Sawyer White. He arrives without notice. He positions himself near the entrances and exits rather than near the work areas he's supposed to be evaluating. Nobody recalls scheduling his visits. Nobody challenges his presence.

The pattern in the disappearances is not random. The targets are people with predictable routines — regular haunts, consistent timing, low variance in behavior. The kind of people who are easy to observe. The kind of people who would not be missed immediately.

What happened to Alfie in 2000 was not a voluntary departure. Maisy knows this. She has known for a long time. She cannot say so directly.

These incidents do not appear connected. The system does not connect them. Nobody is investigating the overlaps. The most dangerous thing about Northside Marsten is not any of its visible dangers.

Core Theme

People are not taken randomly. They are taken because they are easy to predict — and no one protects them.
